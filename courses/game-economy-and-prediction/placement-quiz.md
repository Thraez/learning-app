# Placement quiz

About 10 to 15 minutes. These questions are about specific published studies, not general design knowledge, so expect to guess on many. That is fine: a wrong guess followed by the answer still helps you learn it (finding 7, grade B).

For each question, write or say your answer, mark how sure you were (sure, unsure, guess), then open the answer. Note the result next to the ID. Each question points at the lesson it places you into.

**How to read your result.** Right and sure on every question for a lesson: skim that lesson and do only its scenario. Anything else: do the full lesson. If you get almost everything right and sure, tell me, and the course skips ahead to lessons 7 to 12.

Sources and grades for every answer are in the linked lesson.

### gd-econ/placement/p01 · recall

In a randomized test on about 575,000 new players of a football manager game, new players got 40, 80 or 120 units of premium currency. What happened to day-7 retention in the bigger groups, and did real-money buying drop?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Retention rose about 7% against control in both bigger groups, and buying did not drop. The size of the first purchase rose in the 120 group. Lesson 1.

</details>

### gd-econ/placement/p02 · recall

That same paper's abstract says a bigger money supply leads to inflation. What did the paper actually measure about prices?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Nothing. It measured retention and purchases; the inflation claim is an interpretation. Lesson 1.

</details>

### gd-econ/placement/p03 · recall

Old School RuneScape funded an item sink from a 1% market tax, buying high-level items from players and deleting them. What happened to those items' prices and to their trading volume?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Prices rose (about 7% and 14% in the two rounds) and trading volume did not change meaningfully. Lesson 2.

</details>

### gd-econ/placement/p04 · scenario

A designer proposes a sink that buys rare items off players and destroys them, "so rare items stay affordable". What is wrong with the reasoning?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Buying and destroying items adds demand and cuts supply, so prices go up, not down. That is what happened in RuneScape. Lesson 2.

</details>

### gd-econ/placement/p06 · recall

A churn study pulls data on a fixed day, and many players are still active. What is the name for their unknown outcome, and what kind of model handles it?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Censored data. Survival models, which predict time until churn and treat still-active players as "lasted at least this long". Lesson 4.

</details>

### gd-econ/placement/p07 · scenario

Two studios both report a 30-day churn rate. Why might the numbers not be comparable even for similar games?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Churn is a rule each team picks, such as how many days without a login counts as gone. Different rules give different rates from the same data. Lesson 4.

</details>

### gd-econ/placement/p10 · why

In one or two sentences: why doesn't a churn model that predicts well tell you who should get a retention offer?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

It ranks how likely players are to leave, not how much an offer would change their choice. Some will leave anyway and some would stay anyway; finding the ones an offer moves needs an experiment. Lesson 6.

</details>
