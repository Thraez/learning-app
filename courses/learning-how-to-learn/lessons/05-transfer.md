# Lesson 5: When recall practice carries over to new problems

## Opening question

### learn/transfer/open · open

A review describes a case from an earlier study: people practised recalling a history fact from a question that asked for one part of it, then got a final question about the same fact turned around, so that a different part was the answer ([Pan and Rickard 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151)). The two questions look almost the same. Guess: did practising the first question help with the second?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
No. In that case no transfer was found. The review uses it to show that looking similar is not enough: what mattered was whether the answer stayed the same.
</details>

## The idea

Recall practice does carry over to new questions, but mostly under three conditions. Without them, the effect may be zero.

The source is [Pan and Rickard 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151), a meta-analysis of 192 effect sizes from 122 experiments in 67 articles, about 10,400 people. Each compared practice tests with restudying or rereading, then measured a final test that differed from the practice: a new format, a new question, a new problem. Overall, practice tests beat restudy at d = 0.40 (95% interval 0.31 to 0.50).

[It varied by kind of transfer](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151). Across test formats d = 0.58, and to application and inference questions d = 0.32. Transfer to material seen during study but never tested was d = 0.16, not significant. Problem solving split: problems on medical diagnosis transferred, problems taught with worked examples showed weak or no transfer.

Three things [predicted transfer](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151). First, the final question needed the same or nearly the same answer as the practice question, for example a term asked from its definition and later from a real example. Second, practice was elaborated: broad recall instructions, explaining, or feedback that went beyond the right answer, such as an explanation or rereading the whole text. Third, people did well on the practice test. Feedback that only gave the correct answer was not linked to better transfer.

The limits matter here. [Some studies](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151) with weak results likely went unpublished. After the authors corrected for that, the estimated transfer with neither of the first two factors present was effectively zero; with all three absent it was negative. The three factors come from comparing studies, so the authors call causal claims tentative. Most studies were lab studies, and most tested near transfer; very few crossed subject areas.

## Why

### learn/transfer/why · why

You can recite the definitions of ten design concepts. Why might they still not come to mind in a real design decision? Answer before opening.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>
A design decision rarely asks for the definition itself, so the answer you practised is not the answer you need, and transfer was less likely when the answers differed. The authors offer a candidate reason: when the answer is the same, the route from question to answer you built in practice can be reused. That applying this to design decisions holds is my inference, grade C.
</details>

## Scenario

### learn/transfer/scenario · scenario

You are learning statistics so you can judge A/B test reports at work. Your flashcards ask "What is a p-value?" and "What is a confidence interval?" What would you change, based on this review?

<details>
<summary>Explained answer</summary>
Make practice ask for the answers the reports will need: given a short report, name the problem or the right reading, so practice and use share an answer. After each attempt, read an explanation or the full section, not just the right answer. And learn a topic well before testing yourself on it, since high practice accuracy went with more transfer. The review draws similar lessons itself but warns they rest mostly on lab studies. That a work report counts as an application question is my inference, grade C.
</details>

## Recall

### learn/transfer/r1 · recall

Across 192 effect sizes, how large was transfer from practice tests, compared with restudy?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
d = 0.40, with a 95% interval of 0.31 to 0.50.
</details>

### learn/transfer/r2 · recall

Name the three factors that predicted transfer.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
The same or nearly the same answer on the practice and final test, elaborated practice such as explanatory feedback, and high accuracy on the practice test.
</details>

### learn/transfer/r3 · recall

After correcting for publication bias, what was left of transfer when neither the same-answer factor nor elaborated practice was present?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Effectively nothing: the estimate was about zero.
</details>
