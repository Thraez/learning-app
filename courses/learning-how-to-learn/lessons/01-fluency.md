# Lesson 1: What feels like learning and what is

## Opening question

### learn/fluency/open · open

People studied the painting styles of twelve artists, six paintings each. For six artists the paintings came one after another; for the other six they were mixed in with other artists. Then everyone named the artist of new paintings, and was asked which way of studying had worked better ([Kornell and Bjork 2007](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03194055), describing their own experiment). Guess: what share did better with the mixed paintings, and what share thought the mixed paintings had helped more?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
78% of participants did better on the mixed (spaced) artists, but only 22% thought they had, and they made that judgment after the test, having received feedback on it.
</details>

## The idea

How easy studying feels is a poor gauge of how much you are learning. Sometimes the two point opposite ways.

The main source is [Bjork, Dunlosky and Kornell 2013](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823), a narrative review: the authors summarise studies they chose and pool no numbers. Its abstract says people "often have a faulty mental model" of how they learn and remember, and that their running judgments of their own learning are swayed by current performance and by the sense of fluency.

Fluency here means ease: how readily something comes to mind, or how easy it is to read and take in. The review says this ease [can come from recency, predictability, priming, or cues present while studying that will be gone later](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823), none of which means the thing was learned. It compares these judgments to a speedometer: an inference from a signal, not a direct reading of memory.

The review describes a trivia study by Benjamin and colleagues (1998; I did not open it, so grade B). People rated answers that came to them quickly as more likely to be recalled later, but on the later free-recall test they were most likely to recall the answers they had [thought about for a long time](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823). For a painting study of the same kind, the review's explanation is hedged: blocking [appears to](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823) make the artist's previous painting more fluent, so blocked study is rated as more effective.

Learners also misjudge which techniques work. In a survey of 472 introductory psychology students at UCLA, 68% said they quiz themselves to find out how well they know the material, and only 18% because they learn more that way ([Kornell and Bjork 2007](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03194055), Table 1). 64% said they would put an item aside once convinced they knew it, and 80% said no teacher had taught them how they study.

A long review monograph rated ten techniques. Practice testing and spaced practice got "high utility"; rereading and highlighting got "low", because they [do not consistently boost performance](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266) (Dunlosky et al. 2013, abstract only).

The limits. The survey covers students at one university, and most experiments here reach you through reviews whose original studies I did not open. That it holds for an adult learning a craft over months is my inference, grade C.

## Why

### learn/fluency/why · why

Why does a second read of a chapter feel like progress even when it adds little? Answer before opening.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>
The review says the sense of fluency can come from recency, priming and cues present while studying that will be gone at test time, so it can be high when the memory you will need later is weak. That a second read feels easy for this reason, and that recognising a page is not recalling it with the page closed, is my inference, grade C; the review does not tie rereading to fluency directly.
</details>

## Scenario

### learn/fluency/scenario · scenario

You are learning a new tabletop rules system to run it for friends on Saturday. You read the combat chapter twice on Tuesday and it feels clear. Is that a good sign, and how would you check?

<details>
<summary>Explained answer</summary>
The clear feeling right after a second read is weak evidence: the review names recency as one thing that makes material come to mind easily, and right after reading is when recency is highest (my inference, grade C). A better check is to close the book and try to run a round of combat from memory, then look up what you missed. The review also describes earlier studies where judgments of learning were far more accurate when made after a delay and from a cue alone, so try it again on Thursday rather than straight after reading. Applying lab findings on word pairs to a rulebook is my inference, grade C.
</details>

## Recall

### learn/fluency/r1 · recall

In Kornell and Bjork's survey of 472 students, what share quizzed themselves to check what they knew, and what share because it helps them learn?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
68% to check how well they had learned it, 18% because they learn more that way than by rereading. 9% said they usually do not quiz themselves.
</details>

### learn/fluency/r2 · recall

In the painting study, how many did better with mixed paintings, and how many believed the mixed paintings had helped?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
78% did better with the mixed paintings, and only 22% thought they had, even after the test.
</details>

### learn/fluency/r3 · recall

Which two techniques did Dunlosky and colleagues rate high utility, and how did they rate rereading?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Practice testing and spaced (distributed) practice were rated high utility. Rereading was rated low, along with highlighting, because it does not consistently boost performance.
</details>
