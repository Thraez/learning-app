# Lesson 4: Recall until right, then again another day

## Opening question

### learn/relearning/open · open

Students learned Swahili to English word pairs ([Vaughn, Dunlosky and Rawson 2016](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-016-0606-y)). Some pairs they drilled in one session until they had recalled each one 7 times. Other pairs they recalled once, then relearned in sessions a week apart. Guess: for about the same number of practice tries, which pairs were better remembered a week after their last practice?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
The spread-out pairs, by far. In the second experiment, about 9.1 tries per pair in one session left about 29% recalled a week later. About 8.5 tries per pair, split across a first session and three weekly relearning sessions, left about 72%.
</details>

## The idea

Successive relearning means practising until you get each item right, then practising again until right in later, spaced sessions. That is the definition in [Rawson and Dunlosky 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/09637214221100484), a short review (seven pages). I could open only its abstract, which says the authors review evidence for the method. It says few students use the method in school, and argues that research should measure how much time a method takes, not only the score it gives. The abstract shows no effect sizes, so the size of the benefit comes from the study below.

[Vaughn, Dunlosky and Rawson 2016](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-016-0606-y) ran two experiments with Kent State students, 41 in the first and 26 in the second, four of whom were excluded. Each learned 70 word pairs. In session 1, pairs were drilled until recalled once, twice, and so on up to seven times. Then every pair was relearned to one correct recall in each later session, a week apart. Each relearning session opened with a test, before any restudy, which doubled as a one-week memory check.

Extra drilling in session 1 helped at first. A week later, pairs recalled more times were remembered better. But after relearning, that edge [shrank sharply](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-016-0606-y); the authors call this a relearning-override effect. In the second experiment, recall after a further month was about the same whatever the first-session drilling.

[Relearning was cheap](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-016-0606-y). After the first session, people needed only about 7 minutes per session in the first experiment. In the second, pairs drilled 7 times first took about 15 tries each in all to reach 69% after a month, and pairs drilled twice took about 10 tries to reach 70%. Even 7 correct recalls in one session left under 40% a week later.

The limits. Treat the percentages as approximate. The samples are small, the material is word pairs, and the study shares two authors with the review. The [authors note](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-016-0606-y) that only a handful of studies had tested the method at the time.

## Why

### learn/relearning/why · why

Why would extra drilling in the first session stop mattering once relearning sessions follow? Answer before opening.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>
The results mostly fit an earlier framework (Kornell and others 2015), the authors say: pairs drilled less were more often missed at the start of each relearning session, so they got more tries with the answer shown, and every try with the answer helps memory. So the weaker pairs caught up. The authors say this account does not explain everything, and that the relearned pairs from the less-drilled group may also have been slightly easier.
</details>

## Scenario

### learn/relearning/scenario · scenario

You are learning the shortcuts and node names for a new game engine. Tonight you have an hour and plan to drill each one until you get it right five times in a row, so it "sticks". What would successive relearning have you do instead?

<details>
<summary>Explained answer</summary>
Stop each item at one correct recall tonight. Then come back on later days, start each session by testing yourself before looking anything up, and relearn the misses until each is right once. In the study, the time saved on first-night drilling did more good spent on later sessions. The study spaced sessions a week apart; lesson 3 covers picking the gap. That word-pair results carry over to engine shortcuts is my inference, grade C.
</details>

Practising each item until you get it right, then practising it again until right in later, spaced sessions.

In the second experiment, what did about 9 tries per pair in one session give a week later, against about 8.5 tries per pair spread over four sessions?

</details>

### learn/relearning/r3 · recall

Besides the score, what does the 2022 review argue research on learning methods should measure?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
The time a method takes, treating time on task as an outcome tailored to each learner rather than fixing it.
</details>
