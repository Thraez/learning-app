A review of the research concludes that testing on material you have not learned yet "can often enhance learning" if you study the answers afterwards, though how much varies with the procedure ([Pan and Carpenter 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5), peer-reviewed review).

## Direction

How memory and learning work, and how to use that on anything new. Not a study-tips list. Each lesson takes one finding from cognitive psychology, says how strong the evidence is, and says where it stops applying. The aim is that when you pick up a new subject, at work or for fun, you can plan how to study it and know which of your instincts about learning to distrust.

This course also explains the app you are using. Its lesson shape (a question first, a "why" prompt, recall at the end, missed items again later) comes from the same studies, so you can judge those choices yourself.

## Questions worth exploring

- Why does rereading feel like learning when it does so little?
- What does a practice test do to memory that a second read does not?
- How long should the gap between reviews be, and does it depend on when you need the knowledge?
- When does something you memorised actually come to mind in a new situation?
- Which techniques work for facts, which for judgment, and which for telling similar things apart?
- How much does what you already know, or how much you practise, decide how fast you learn?

## How strong the evidence is

Good in places, thin in others. Seven of the lessons rest on meta-analyses, from 29 studies (spacing, lesson 3) to 8,776 effect sizes (prior knowledge, lesson 10). Several of those abstracts name the limits themselves: interleaving fails for words, transfer often vanishes after a bias correction, and deliberate practice explains little in professions. My reading, not checked study by study: most of this research measures test scores after hours to months, in labs and classrooms, so applying it to learning a professional skill over years is often an inference, and the lessons will say so. Two topics are thin: curiosity (lesson 9) rests on one brain imaging study, and successive relearning (lesson 4) on a short review whose abstract gives no figures, plus one paper with two small experiments on word pairs.

Each source carries two labels. The **grade** says how much of it I read: A read in the paper itself (abstract or full text), B a secondary summary or a figure the paper gives without its data, C my inference. The **tier** says what kind of source it is: 1 peer-reviewed paper or review, 2 a preprint or report by named researchers (always labelled not peer-reviewed), 3 a studio's own data report. Press releases, blogs, slide decks and trade-press write-ups are not used for facts. Every claim links to its source at the sentence.

What each lesson rests on:

1. A review concludes that people often hold a faulty model of how they learn, and that their judgments of their own learning are swayed by how fluent the material feels ([Bjork, Dunlosky and Kornell 2013](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823)). In a survey of 472 students, 68% said they quiz themselves to check what they know and only 18% because they learn more that way ([Kornell and Bjork 2007](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03194055)). A large review rated practice testing and spaced practice high utility, and rereading and highlighting low ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)).
2. Across 159 effect sizes, testing beat restudy at g = 0.50; with feedback g = 0.73, without g = 0.39 ([Rowland 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559)).
3. In a study of more than 1,350 people, the best gap between reviews grew as the final test moved further away, but shrank as a share of it ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x)). The best gaps tested were [1 day for a one-week delay and 21 days for a one-year delay](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x), from the in-press manuscript. A meta-analysis of 29 studies found spaced retrieval beat massed retrieval (g = 0.74) and found no significant difference between expanding and even gaps ([Latimier, Peyre and Ramus 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)). The authors' [preprint](https://osf.io/kzy7u), which is not peer-reviewed, says 0.74 is the figure after a correction for publication bias and gives 1.02 before it.
4. Successive relearning means practising until each item is right, then doing that again in later sessions ([Rawson and Dunlosky 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/09637214221100484)). The abstract gives no effect sizes, so the lesson takes its figures from two experiments on word pairs ([Vaughn, Dunlosky and Rawson 2016](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-016-0606-y)).
5. Recall practice transferred to new contexts at d = 0.40 across 192 effect sizes, including to application and inference questions. After correcting for publication bias there was often no transfer unless the later test asked for the same or nearly the same answer, retrieval practice was elaborated, or the first test went well ([Pan and Rickard 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151)).
6. Self-explanation prompts gave g = 0.55 across 69 effect sizes ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x)).
7. Interleaving gave g = 0.42 overall, more for similar categories, and blocking beat it for words (g = -0.39) ([Brunmair and Richter 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209)).
8. Pretesting often helps ([Pan and Carpenter 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5)), and in five experiments learners still believed reading worked better after trying both ([Pan and Rivers 2023](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-022-01392-1)).
9. In one fMRI study, people remembered information they were curious about better, and also incidental material shown while curious ([Gruber, Gelman and Ranganath 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.08.060)). One study; the abstract does not give the sample size.
10. Across 8,776 effect sizes, prior knowledge correlated with later knowledge at about .53 but with normalised learning gain at about -.06, with a prediction interval from -.69 to .62 ([Simonsmeier et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2021.1939700)).
11. Deliberate practice explained 26% of the variance in performance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and under 1% in professions ([Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535810)). That is about differences between people, not about whether practice helps you.
12. A synthesis lesson: you plan the study of a subject you choose, using retrieval, spacing and self-explanation. No new source; grade C.

Four of these are narrative reviews rather than meta-analyses (Bjork, Dunlosky and Kornell 2013; Dunlosky et al. 2013; Rawson and Dunlosky 2022; Pan and Carpenter 2023): the authors summarise studies they chose, without pooling numbers. The lessons say so where they lean on one.

"Tier 1 unverified" marks three review journals whose review process I could not confirm: Annual Review of Psychology (its pages blocked automated access), Psychological Science in the Public Interest (its page says its articles are commissioned and does not say how they are reviewed) and Current Directions in Psychological Science (its page speaks of peer review only for some manuscripts). They are scholarly journals and I treat them as tier 1, but that is my judgment until someone opens each journal's policy page.

Considered and not used: the Adesope 2017 practice-testing meta-analysis, whose effect sizes could not be read in an earlier pass and which was not reopened.
