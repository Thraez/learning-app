# Course check: learning-how-to-learn

Date: 2026-11-19

Method. For each lesson I took the one or two main findings from the front matter, "Opening question" and "The idea", then searched for meta-analyses, replications, classroom studies and null or contrary results. Tools: WebSearch to find papers; Europe PMC, Crossref and Springer pages for abstracts; full texts where a free copy opened (author or university repository copies, named in words below, with the DOI as the link). Limits: many papers were read at the abstract only, because the body is paywalled. Where I only searched an author's text for the passages I needed, I say which sections. A primary study that is already pooled in the lesson's own meta-analysis is not counted as outside evidence; I checked this against the reference lists of Pan and Rickard 2018 and Yang et al. 2021, and the introduction of the Latimier preprint. Search snippets were never used as evidence.

## learn/fluency

**The finding as the lesson states it.** How easy studying feels is a poor gauge of learning, and the two can point opposite ways. In a painting study, 78% did better with mixed (interleaved) paintings but only 22% thought so. Ease can come from recency, priming or study cues. Students mostly self-test to check knowledge, not to learn.

**Searched.** Meta-analyses of the font-size effect on judgments of learning (JOLs) and of delayed JOLs; replications of the painting study; interleaving meta-analysis; metacognitive beliefs about interleaving; replications of the Kornell and Bjork 2007 survey.

**Found.**

- [Kornell, Bjork 2008, Psychological Science](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x). Tier 1, abstract. The original painting experiments: interleaving helped induction, yet people rated massing as more effective even after their own test showed the opposite. Same authors as the lesson's source, so not independent.
- [Kornell, Castel, Eich, Bjork 2010, Psychology and Aging](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017807). Tier 1, abstract. Replicated the spacing benefit for paintings in young and older adults, and again people rated massing better for induction. Same research group.
- [Yan, Bjork, Bjork 2016, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000177). Tier 1, abstract. Learners keep believing blocking is better even after benefiting from interleaving. The authors name three causes together: the sense of fluency, beliefs learners bring to the task, and a belief that one learns differently from others. Debiasing had only modest effects until test results for the two schedules were separated. Same research group; it adds prior beliefs as a cause next to fluency.
- [Kirk-Johnson, Galla, Fraundorf 2019, Cognitive Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2019.101237). Tier 1, abstract. Independent lab. Across interleaving and retrieval practice, learners who found a strategy more effortful rated it less effective and chose it less, while the effortful choice went with better long-term retention in their third study. Supports the lesson's point that felt difficulty misleads.
- [Rhodes, Castel 2008, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0013684). Tier 1, abstract. Larger font raised JOLs with little link to recall; the effect disappeared when large words were made harder to read, which the authors take as a fluency effect. Supports the lesson. Castel co-authored Kornell et al. 2010, so not fully independent of the Bjork group.
- [Undorf, Zimdahl 2019, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0000571). Tier 1, abstract. Across font sizes from 6 to 500 point, JOLs rose steadily with size even where very large fonts lowered measured perceptual fluency. The authors conclude beliefs about font size, not perceptual fluency, drive the effect. Contrary on mechanism, not on the illusion itself.
- [Chang, Brainerd 2022, Metacognition and Learning](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-021-09287-3). Tier 1, abstract. Meta-analysis of the font-size effect: a small-to-moderate effect on JOLs and also a small but significant effect on memory, so JOLs and memory are not fully dissociated. Their working account ties the memory effect to fluency but not the JOL effect. Supports "judgments are swayed by surface cues", but weakens "fluency" as the explanation.
- [Rhodes, Tauber 2011, Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021705). Tier 1, abstract. Meta-analysis of 112 effect sizes: delaying JOLs raised their relative accuracy a lot (g = 0.93). Supports the lesson's scenario advice to judge again after a delay.
- [Brunmair, Richter 2019, Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209). Tier 1, abstract. Meta-analysis of 59 studies: interleaving beat blocking overall (g = 0.42) and most for paintings (g = 0.67), but blocking beat interleaving for words (g = -0.39), and results for expository texts were not significant. A boundary on the painting example.
- [Hartwig, Dunlosky 2012, Psychonomic Bulletin and Review](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-011-0181-y). Tier 1, abstract. A survey of 324 undergraduates that builds on Kornell and Bjork 2007. Self-testing and rereading were both positively linked to GPA (a correlation). Dunlosky is an author of the lesson's main review, so this is partly the same group.

**Verdict: supported.** An independent lab and a meta-analysis agree that people's judgments of learning follow surface cues and felt effort rather than what they will remember. What is mixed is the cause: for font size, a meta-analysis and Undorf and Zimdahl point to beliefs rather than fluency, and the Bjork group itself names beliefs as a second cause. Stands alone: the survey figures (68% self-test to check, 18% to learn). Hartwig and Dunlosky's abstract says earlier surveys found self-testing used largely for monitoring, but gives no figures of its own on that.

**Lesson edit called for.** The lesson should say that beliefs about what works, not only the feel of fluency, drive these misjudgments, and that the interleaving benefit shown with paintings reversed for words in a meta-analysis.

## learn/testing

**The finding as the lesson states it.** Across 159 effect sizes, a practice test beat restudy (g = 0.50). With feedback g = 0.73, without g = 0.39; without feedback and with half or fewer right, no reliable gain (g = 0.03). Recall practice beat recognition practice.

**Searched.** Later testing-effect meta-analyses; classroom reviews; feedback as a moderator; recall against recognition formats; complex materials; preregistered tests of retrieval against restudy.

**Found.**

- [Adesope, Trevisan, Sundararajan 2017, Review of Educational Research](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306). Tier 1, abstract only (the free copy was blocked). Meta-analysis: practice tests beat restudy and all other comparison conditions, with effects moderated by test features, participants and study methods. Supports the main effect. Its feedback figures are not in the abstract; as Yang et al. 2021 report them, Adesope found no moderating effect of feedback (with feedback g = 0.63, without g = 0.60).
- [Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu, Shanks 2021, Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309). Tier 1, full text opened (a third-party hosted copy of the published PDF); read the abstract, the introduction on earlier meta-analyses, the moderator results and the discussion of format. Classroom meta-analysis of 222 studies and 48,478 students: quizzing raised achievement, g = 0.499. Feedback mattered (g = 0.537 with, 0.374 without), agreeing with Rowland. But recall and recognition quizzes did not differ, with feedback (recall 0.541, recognition 0.607) or without (0.520, 0.518), and the authors read this as test difficulty playing little role in classrooms. The authors report little risk of publication bias.
- [Agarwal, Nunes, Blunt 2021, Educational Psychology Review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09595-9). Tier 1, abstract. Systematic review of 50 classroom experiments (49 effect sizes, n = 5,374): 57% showed medium or large benefits of retrieval practice, across levels, subjects, delays and formats. Only 6% were run in non-WEIRD countries.
- [van Gog, Sweller 2015, Educational Psychology Review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9310-x). Tier 1, abstract. Introduction to a special issue arguing that the testing effect shrinks, and may vanish, as material grows more complex. A proposed boundary condition.
- [Karpicke, Aue 2015, Educational Psychology Review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9309-3). Tier 1, abstract. A reply: complexity was not measured or manipulated in the null studies, several positive studies with complex material were left out, and the worked-example data show a small positive effect. So the complexity boundary is contested.
- [Higham, Fastrich, Potts, Murayama, Pickering, Hadwin 2023, Educational Psychology Review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09809-2). Tier 1, abstract. Three preregistered online experiments with Swahili to English pairs. When learners had to rate their memory on every trial, restudy beat retrieval; with the rating removed (Experiment 3), retrieval beat restudy in all conditions. A boundary condition, not a failure of the main effect.

**Verdict: supported.** Two later meta-analyses and a classroom systematic review find a medium benefit of testing over restudy. Their study pools likely overlap Rowland's in part (Yang's is classroom-only, Rowland's mostly lab), so they are not fully independent. Two of the lesson's secondary claims are mixed: Adesope found no feedback effect where Rowland and Yang did, and the classroom meta-analysis found no recall over recognition advantage. Stands alone: the opening-question result (no feedback, half or fewer right, g = 0.03). I searched Yang's full text for a moderator on quiz accuracy and found none; no other source I opened tests it.

**Lesson edit called for.** The lesson should say that the recall over recognition advantage comes from Rowland's mostly lab studies, and that a classroom meta-analysis of 222 studies found no difference between recall and recognition quizzes.

## learn/spacing

**The finding as the lesson states it.** There is no single best gap: the best gap grows with the delay to the test but shrinks as a share of it. In Cepeda et al. 2008 the best gaps tested were 1, 11, 21 and 21 days for delays of 7, 35, 70 and 350 days. Growing gaps did no better than even gaps (Latimier et al.).

**Searched.** Spacing meta-analyses; replications of Cepeda 2008 and 2009; classroom tests of gap against delay; L2 spacing meta-analyses; expanding against equal schedules; classroom spacing meta-analyses.

**Found.**

- [Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted, Rohrer 2006, Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354). Tier 1, abstract. Meta-analysis of 317 experiments: the gap giving the best retention grew as the retention interval grew. Agrees, but it is largely the same authors as Cepeda 2008.
- [Cepeda, Coburn, Rohrer, Wixted, Mozer, Pashler 2009, Experimental Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1027/1618-3169.56.4.236). Tier 1, abstract. Two lab studies with delays up to 6 months: recall first rose sharply with the gap, then slowly fell. Same lab.
- [Kim, Wong-Kee-You, Wiseheart, Rosenbaum 2019, Behavior Research Methods](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-018-1184-7). Tier 1, abstract, read from the full-text PDF on the author's York University page. Workplace training records from 10,514 people: the best spacing between retrievals grew with the retention interval. Observational, not randomized. Not independent: Melody Wiseheart's York publication page says "formerly Nicholas J. Cepeda", the first author of the lesson's source.
- [Küpper-Tetzel, Erdfelder, Dickhäuser 2013, Instructional Science](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-013-9285-2). Tier 1, abstract. Independent classroom study. Sixth-graders relearned English vocabulary after 0, 1 or 10 days and were tested 7 or 35 days later. At 7 days a 1-day lag was best; at 35 days both 1 and 10 days helped. Supports the direction: the best lag depends on the delay.
- [Kim, Webb 2022, Language Learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12479). Tier 1, abstract. Meta-analysis of 48 L2 experiments (N = 3,411): spacing had a medium-to-large effect; shorter spacing matched longer spacing on immediate tests but was less effective on delayed tests; equal and expanding spacing were statistically equivalent. Supports both the direction and Latimier's expanding result. Some studies may overlap with Latimier's.
- [Rogers, Nakata, Chiu 2025, Studies in Second Language Acquisition](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263124000706). Tier 1, full text opened (open access, PolyU repository copy); read the abstract, introduction, training results and the start of the discussion. Online replication of Cepeda 2009 Experiment 1 at one 10-day delay. With covariates controlled, all spaced gaps beat massed and the 4, 7 and 14 day gaps beat 1 day; in the plain ANOVA only the 2-day gap beat massed. So the result depended on the analysis. The authors describe the wider L2 spacing literature as mixed. One delay only, so it cannot test whether the best gap depends on the delay.
- [Mawson, Kang 2025, Behavioral Sciences](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs15060771). Tier 1, abstract. Classroom meta-analysis of 31 effect sizes: distributed beat massed practice (d = 0.54), with larger effects in studies with longer retention intervals. A full moderator analysis was not possible.

**Verdict: supported.** The direction, that the best gap grows with the delay, is backed by a meta-analysis, an independent classroom study and an L2 meta-analysis. The specific best gaps and percentages come from one experiment and stand alone; much of the rest is the same lab.

**Lesson edit called for.** The lesson could add that an independent classroom study found the same direction (best lag 1 day for a 7-day test; 1 and 10 days both helped for a 35-day test), while the exact gaps it quotes have no outside replication.

## learn/relearning

**The finding as the lesson states it.** Successive relearning (recall until right, then again in later sessions) beat one-session drilling by far: about 72% against 29% a week later in Vaughn et al. 2016 Experiment 2. Extra drilling in the first session stopped mattering once relearning followed (the relearning-override effect).

**Searched.** Successive relearning reviews and meta-analyses; classroom studies; independent replications; boundary conditions; null results.

**Found.**

- [Rawson, Dunlosky 2011, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023956). Tier 1, abstract. Three experiments, 533 students: the effects of first-session criterion and relearning were subadditive; the criterion effect was strong before relearning and faded as relearning increased, at small cost in extra trials. The same pattern as the override effect, from the same lab.
- [Rawson, Dunlosky, Sciartelli 2013, Educational Psychology Review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-013-9240-4). Tier 1, abstract. Two classroom experiments: successive relearning improved course exam scores and long-term retention. The abstract gives no numbers. Same lab.
- [Rawson, Dunlosky, Janes 2020, Educational Psychology Review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09528-y). Tier 1, abstract. Three experiments, 431 students learning probability problems: successive relearning beat single-session learning only slightly (pooled d = 0.28). The authors call this a potential boundary condition for math procedures. Same lab, but a real limit.
- [Higham, Zengel, Bartlett, Hadwin 2022, Journal of Educational Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000693). Tier 1, full text opened (accepted manuscript, Liverpool Hope University repository); read the abstract, method and criterial-test results. Independent lab, introductory psychology course, within-person design. The abstract says exposure duration was controlled; the data show relearning got less exposure per session than restudy (about 29.5 s against 35 s per question), so exposure does not explain the gain. At end of semester, relearning beat restudy on repeated questions (55% against 41%, d = 0.705). For transfer questions (a different blank in the same sentence) and new questions on the same topics, the gains were small and not significant (p = .051 and p = .054). The comparison was restudy, not one-session drilling, so it does not test the override effect.

**Verdict: supported.** The core benefit holds across several experiments and one independent classroom study. Two limits: almost all evidence is from the Rawson and Dunlosky lab, and the override effect has no test outside it.

**Lesson edit called for.** The lesson should say that the same lab found only a small advantage when the material was math procedures (d = 0.28), and that the one independent classroom study tested relearning against restudy, not against one-session drilling.

## learn/transfer

**The finding as the lesson states it.** Practice tests beat restudy on transfer tests (d = 0.40), varying by kind of transfer. Untested material seen during study showed d = 0.16, not significant. Transfer was predicted by the same answer, elaborated practice and high practice accuracy; with the first two absent, corrected transfer was effectively zero.

**Searched.** Later meta-analyses of transfer of retrieval practice; classroom studies; null results for inference questions; reviews.

**Found.**

- [Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu, Shanks 2021, Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309). Tier 1, full text opened, sections as above. In classrooms, quizzing helped rephrased questions (g = 0.558) and untested material (g = 0.321, significant). The authors say this conflicts with Pan and Rickard's null for untested material. They offer more effects (43 against 17), related lecture content against the unrelated stimuli in many lab studies, and repeated study-test cycles in classes as reasons.
- [Higham, Zengel, Bartlett, Hadwin 2022, Journal of Educational Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000693). Tier 1, full text opened, sections as above. Not part of Pan and Rickard. When the final question removed a different word from a practised sentence, so the answer changed, the relearning advantage was small and not significant (53% against 50%, p = .051). Fits the lesson's point that transfer is weak when the answer differs.
- [Agarwal, Nunes, Blunt 2021, Educational Psychology Review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09595-9). Tier 1, abstract. Classroom review: retrieval practice helped across retrieval and final-test formats. Agrees in general terms; it does not test the three conditions.
- [Carpenter 2012, Current Directions in Psychological Science](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721412452728). Tier 1, abstract. Short review of a "small but growing" set of studies reporting transfer across time, formats and domains. Earlier and narrative; its studies likely sit inside Pan and Rickard.
- [Butler 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019902) and [Tran, Rohrer, Pashler 2015](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-014-0646-x). Tier 1, abstracts. Butler found transfer to new inference questions, including other domains; Tran et al. found better recall but no gain on deductive inference questions. Both are starred as included studies in Pan and Rickard's reference list, so they are not outside evidence.

**Verdict: supported.** A larger classroom meta-analysis agrees that testing transfers to new questions, and an independent classroom study fits the "same answer" condition. The one conflict is on untested material, where the classroom meta-analysis found a significant effect. Stands alone: the elaboration and practice-accuracy conditions, and the bias-corrected "effectively zero" estimate. No source I opened outside Pan and Rickard tests them.

**Lesson edit called for.** The lesson should say that a classroom meta-analysis of 43 effects found a significant benefit for untested material (g = 0.321), against the not-significant 0.16 it quotes, and that the authors link this to related course content.
