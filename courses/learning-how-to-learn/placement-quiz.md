# Placement quiz

About 10 to 15 minutes. These questions are about specific published findings, so expect to guess on some. That is the point: a review of the research finds that testing on material you have not learned yet can often help you learn it, if you study the answers afterwards ([Pan and Carpenter 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5), peer-reviewed review).

For each question, write or say your answer, mark how sure you were (sure, unsure, guess), then open the answer. Each question points at the planned lesson it places you into. No lessons are written yet, so the numbers refer to the lesson list on the course page.

**How to read your result.** Items marked `recall` ask for figures from one paper, so they are a pretest, not a placement. Place yourself with the `scenario` and `why` items: right and sure on a lesson's item means that lesson can be shortened to its limits and scenario. Lesson 12 is a planning exercise and has no quiz item.

### learn/placement/p01 · scenario

A colleague rereads their notes three times before a design review and says the material "feels solid now". What does the research say about that feeling, and what would be a better check?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>
The feeling is a poor gauge. People often hold a faulty model of how they learn, their sense of how well they know something is swayed by how fluent the material feels, and the benefits of rereading are "modest at best" ([Bjork, Dunlosky and Kornell 2013](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823)). A large review rated rereading low utility and practice testing high ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)). A better check is to close the notes and try to recall or explain the points. Lesson 1.
</details>

### learn/placement/p02 · recall

A survey of 472 university students asked why they test themselves. About what share said they do it because they learn more that way, rather than to check what they know? Pick one: 18%, 40%, 70%, 90%.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
18%. Most, 68%, said they quiz themselves to figure out how well they have learned, and 9% said they usually don't quiz themselves. The students were in an introductory psychology class at UCLA ([Kornell and Bjork 2007](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03194055)). Lesson 1.
</details>

### learn/placement/p03 · why

You do a practice quiz and then close the app without looking at the answers. Why might that waste most of the benefit, and when does it matter most?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>
Seeing the right answer after a try is where much of the gain comes from. In a meta-analysis, testing beat restudy at g = 0.73 with feedback and g = 0.39 without, and without feedback there was no reliable gain when 50% or fewer of the first answers were right (g = 0.03) ([Rowland 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559)). So it matters most on new or hard material, where you get many answers wrong. Lesson 2.
</details>

### learn/placement/p04 · recall

In the same meta-analysis, was the testing effect bigger when the final test came a day or more later, or when it came sooner?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
A day or more later: g = 0.69, against g = 0.41 for under a day ([Rowland 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559)). Lesson 2.
</details>

### learn/placement/p05 · scenario

You need to remember a set of facts for a test in one week. A friend needs the same facts in one year. Should you both put the same gap between your first study and your review?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>
No. In a study of more than 1,350 people, the best gap grew as the final test moved further away, but shrank as a share of the delay: about 20 to 40% of a one-week delay, and about 5 to 10% of a one-year delay ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x)). So you review after about one and a half to three days, and your friend waits about three to five weeks. Lesson 3.
</details>

### learn/placement/p06 · why

Many flashcard apps make each gap between reviews longer than the last. Does the evidence say expanding gaps beat even ones?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>
Not on average. A meta-analysis of 29 studies found spacing retrieval out clearly beat massing it (g = 0.74), but found no significant difference between expanding and even gaps (g = 0.034). Expanding gaps did better when items were tested more times ([Latimier, Peyre and Ramus 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)). Lesson 3.
</details>

### learn/placement/p07 · scenario

You answer a hard question right once today, after two misses. Is that item learned? What would successive relearning have you do next?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>
Not yet. Successive relearning means practising an item until you get it right, then doing that again in later, spaced sessions ([Rawson and Dunlosky 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/09637214221100484)). So it comes back another day, and you keep at it until right again. The review argues for measuring how much time that takes, not only the score. Lesson 4.
</details>

### learn/placement/p08 · why

You learn the definitions of ten design concepts on flashcards and can recall all of them. Why might they still not come to mind when a real design decision needs them?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>
Recall practice does carry over, but under conditions. A meta-analysis found transfer at d = 0.40 overall, including to application and inference questions. Three things strongly raised the chance of it: the later test asking for the same or nearly the same answer, elaborated retrieval practice, and doing well on the first test. After correcting for publication bias, there was often no transfer when none of the three was present ([Pan and Rickard 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151)). A design decision rarely asks for the definition itself, so bare definition flashcards may not carry over; that last step is my inference. Lesson 5.
</details>

### learn/placement/p09 · scenario

You are reading the method section of a paper and want to understand it, not just get through it. What one habit has meta-analytic support, and how big is the effect?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>
Explain to yourself why each step is done and how it connects to the rest. Prompts to self-explain gave g = 0.55 across 69 effect sizes, and the authors call it a potentially powerful intervention across a range of instructional conditions ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x)). Lesson 6.
</details>

### learn/placement/p10 · scenario

You are learning two things from examples: telling apart the painting styles of six similar concept artists, and a list of words in a new language. For which should you mix the examples together rather than study them in blocks?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>
Mix the paintings, block the words. In a meta-analysis, interleaving helped overall (g = 0.42) and most for paintings (g = 0.67), more when categories were similar to each other, while in studies using words blocking did better (g = -0.39). Results for expository texts were unclear, so the paper urges caution there ([Brunmair and Richter 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209)). Lesson 7.
</details>

### learn/placement/p11 · why

Every lesson in this app opens with a question you probably can't answer yet. Why, and why might it feel like a worse way to learn even when it works?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>
Testing before teaching can often help you learn the answer afterwards, though the size varies with the procedure ([Pan and Carpenter 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5)). Across five experiments, learners still believed reading was more effective even after trying both, and needed support before they saw the benefit ([Pan and Rivers 2023](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-022-01392-1)). Lesson 8.
</details>

### learn/placement/p12 · why

Why might letting you choose which topic comes next help you remember it, and how strong is the evidence for that?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>
In one fMRI study, people remembered information they were curious about better, both right away and a day later, and also remembered incidental material shown to them while curious ([Gruber, Gelman and Ranganath 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.08.060)). It is one study, and the abstract does not give the sample size, so treating choice as a memory aid is a reasonable bet, not an established effect. Lesson 9.
</details>

### learn/placement/p13 · scenario

Someone says: "You have a degree in this area, so you'll pick up the advanced material faster than a newcomer." What does a meta-analysis of prior knowledge say?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>
Prior knowledge predicts how much you know afterwards (correlation about .53) but not how much you gain: the correlation with normalised gain was about -.06, with a prediction interval from about -.69 to .62. The effect can be positive, negative or nothing, depending on conditions not yet mapped ([Simonsmeier et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2021.1939700)). Lesson 10.
</details>

### learn/placement/p14 · why

A lead says that focused practice is what separates great designers from the rest. In a meta-analysis, how much of the variance in performance did deliberate practice explain in professions, compared with games, and what does that figure not tell you?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>
Under 1% in professions, against 26% in games ([Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535810)). It is about differences between people, so it does not say whether practice makes a given person better. Lesson 11.
</details>
